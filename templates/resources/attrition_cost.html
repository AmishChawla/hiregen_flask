How the formula works

The calculator estimates how many employees will leave in a year, then multiplies that by the fully loaded cost of replacing each one — direct replacement cost (recruiting, interviewing, lost productivity) plus training investment.

Attrition Cost formula Attrition Cost = (Total Employees × Turnover Rate) × (Average Salary × Replacement Cost Multiplier + Training Investment per Hire)

The replacement cost multiplier is the part most teams get wrong. It's meant to capture everything beyond training: job ad spend, recruiter and interviewer time, the productivity gap while a seat is vacant, and lower output while the new hire ramps up. A low-complexity, high-volume role might use 0.3–0.5; a specialized or leadership role often justifies 0.75–2.0.

Example: A 250-person company with 15% annual turnover, a $65,000 average salary, a 0.5 replacement multiplier, and $2,000 in training investment per hire loses roughly $1,293,750 a year to attrition — try your own numbers in the calculator above.

The hidden costs most teams miss

The direct line items — job ads, agency fees, sign-on bonuses — are the easiest to track and usually the smallest part of the total. The larger cost tends to sit in what's harder to see:

  • Vacancy drag: unfilled work that still has to get done, absorbed by the remaining team or left undone.
  • Ramp-up productivity gap: new hires typically operate below full productivity for weeks or months.
  • Manager and interviewer time: hours spent screening, interviewing, and onboarding are rarely costed against the role.
  • Knowledge and relationship loss: client relationships, tribal knowledge, and process context that leave with the employee.
  • Team morale and secondary attrition: a departure that triggers more departures on the same team.

How to reduce attrition costs

  1. Fix the front door. A large share of early turnover traces back to mis-set expectations or weak role fit during screening. Structured interviews and clearer job previews reduce early exits.
  2. Invest in the first 90 days. Structured onboarding with clear milestones consistently correlates with longer tenure than ad hoc onboarding.
  3. Train managers, not just employees. Manager quality is one of the most cited drivers of voluntary turnover — equip managers to run stay interviews and give real-time feedback.
  4. Build visible career paths. Internal mobility keeps tenured employees rather than losing them to external moves for growth.
  5. Track leading indicators. Engagement surveys, manager 1:1 cadence, and exit interview themes flag risk before it shows up in turnover numbers.
  6. Use hiring data to close the loop. A recruitment CRM or applicant tracking system can show which sourcing channels and hiring managers consistently produce longer-tenured hires, so you can double down on what's working.
