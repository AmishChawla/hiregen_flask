How the formula works

Recruitment ROI compares what a hire is worth against what it cost to bring them on, discounted for the real risk they don't stay long enough to deliver that value.

Recruitment ROI formula ROI (%) = ((Revenue × Retention − Cost per Hire) ÷ Cost per Hire) × 100

A result above 0% means the hire's adjusted value exceeded what it cost to hire them. A result of 100% means the value generated was double the hiring cost. A negative result means the hire cost more than the retention-adjusted value it produced — usually a sign of high cost per hire, low retention, or an overestimated revenue contribution.

Example: A $4,500 cost per hire against $30,000 in annual revenue contribution at 85% retention produces a 466.7% ROI — well above the 150%–400% range considered strong.

Estimating revenue contribution and retention

These two inputs carry the most judgment in the formula, so it's worth being deliberate about them.

  • Revenue contribution: for quota-carrying or directly revenue-generating roles, use actual attributable revenue or bookings. For other roles, a common proxy is a multiple of fully loaded salary tied to expected productivity or output value.
  • Retention rate: use your own historical retention data for similar roles at the relevant tenure milestone — for example, 12-month retention — rather than a company-wide average, since retention varies significantly by role and seniority.

How to improve recruitment ROI

  1. Lower cost per hire where it doesn't hurt quality. Shift spend toward higher-yield channels like referrals to reduce the denominator without sacrificing fit.
  2. Invest in early retention. Retention has an outsized effect on ROI since it directly scales the value side of the formula — strong onboarding and manager enablement pay off here.
  3. Prioritize roles with measurable impact. Concentrate recruiting investment on roles where revenue or productivity contribution is clear and trackable.
  4. Re-run the numbers by role type. A single company-wide ROI figure hides which roles are actually driving (or dragging down) your return.
