Building a weekly hiring dashboard

Module 10, Lesson 5  ·  3 min read ·  Updated 21 September 2026

Module 10 · Lesson 5

In short

  • A weekly dashboard has one job: tell you what to do this week.
  • Five numbers is enough. The most useful is who is going cold.
  • Always show the count next to the percentage.

A weekly dashboard has one job: tell you what needs attention this week. Anything that does not change a decision belongs in the monthly report, or nowhere.

Five numbers

NumberAnswersWhat you do about it
Open jobs with nobody in the processWhich searches have stalled?Go looking, or reset expectations
Candidates waiting on someone, by personWho is holding things up?A specific nudge to a specific person
Days since last contactWho is going cold?Contact them today
Offers out and their statusWhat is about to close or fall through?Step in before a refusal
Hires so far against the planAre we on track?The conversation with the business

The third one is the most valuable on the list. Every recruiting team loses people to silence, and almost none can name who is currently being lost that way.

Rules for a dashboard people actually use

  • Every number shows its count. "62%" is not a fact. "13 of 21" is.
  • Every chart says what dates it covers. Without it, people compare different time periods.
  • No jargon. Hiring managers read this.
  • Nothing needs you to explain it. If a number only makes sense when you are in the room, it will be misread in every meeting you miss.
  • It fits on one screen. A dashboard that scrolls is a report.
  • Flag small samples. Anything under about ten should show a count, not a percentage.

Building it from exports

Most built-in reporting is either too rigid or too slow to set up. A weekly export into a spreadsheet, with the sums already built, is usually the fastest route to something useful and the easiest to change.

  1. One export: candidates, with job, stage, date they entered that stage, source, owner, status.
  2. One export: jobs, with the date opened, target start date, status.
  3. Sums on a separate sheet, so a fresh export never overwrites your formulas.
  4. The dashboard reads from the sums sheet, never from the raw data.

That third rule saves more trouble than anything else here. The most common way spreadsheet dashboards die is someone pasting new data over the formulas.

Keeping it current

Schedule the export if your system can. If not, pick a fixed time — Monday morning — and always do it then. A dashboard refreshed irregularly is worse than none, because nobody can tell whether they are looking at last week or last month.

Show the refresh date clearly. That is the difference between a number being trusted and being questioned.

The test

Does the dashboard change what you do on Monday? If it only tells you how last quarter went, it is a report — useful, but it belongs somewhere else and on a different schedule.
