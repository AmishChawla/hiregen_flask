Building a match profile that reflects the scorecard

Module 5, Lesson 2  ·  4 min read ·  Updated 21 September 2026

Module 5 · Lesson 2

In short

  • Build your match profile from your scorecard, not from your job advert.
  • Do not build it from your best employee's CV. You will just hire people like them.
  • Test it on one strong and one weak candidate before using it for real.

A match profile is the description the software compares candidates against. Build it from the job and it finds people who can do the work. Build it from a person and it finds people who resemble that person.

Start from the scorecard, not the job advert

Your advert was written to attract people. It contains company description, benefits and tone — none of which should affect a ranking.

Feeding the advert in as your match profile is the most common setup mistake. It produces rankings partly driven by how much a CV sounds like marketing copy.

The right input is the scorecard from Module 2 — specifically the items you marked as checkable from a CV. Those are already written as things you could watch someone do, with notes on what counts as proof. That is exactly the shape a match profile needs.

Not all evidence is worth the same

Most tools treat everything in a CV equally. It is not.

What it saysWorthWhy
Described work with specifics — scale, numbers, outcomeHighestHard to make up, easy to ask about at interview
Tools named inside a project descriptionHighShows use, not just exposure
Job titlesMediumEvery company names them differently
A list of skillsLowSelf-declared, unverifiable, easy to stuff
EducationLow for most jobsWeak predictor, and closely tied to background
The summary at the topLowestWritten to match whatever was advertised

If your tool lets you weight sections, push weight towards described work and away from skill lists and summaries. If it does not, know that part of your ranking is measuring who wrote the better opening paragraph.

The "just like our best employee" trap

The easy way to build a profile is to take your best team member's CV and use it as the target. It feels sensible. It also builds in everything incidental about that person: the kind of company they came from, how they got into the field, the way they write.

You end up selecting for resemblance rather than ability — including things you never intended and cannot see in the results.

Test for it directly. Ask: if someone had done all the same work at a different kind of company, by a different route, would this profile still find them? If not, rewrite it around what was done rather than where.

Two tests, ten minutes

One strong, one weak. Pick two people whose suitability you are sure about — ideally past applicants for a similar job. Run both. If the strong one does not rank clearly above the weak one, your profile is not measuring what you think.

The shape test. Take your top 20 and look at their careers as shapes rather than contents. Are they all continuously employed? All from one industry? All from big companies? All following the same route in?

A profile that only finds one shape of career is selecting on the shape — and career shape reflects circumstances far more than ability.

Expect to rewrite it

Two or three attempts is normal. The usual corrections, in order of how often they come up: remove a requirement that was really a preference, swap a job-title rule for a what-they-did rule, cut the weight on skill lists, and add something that separates your strong example from your weak one when the first run put them close together.

Keep the profile with the job

Store it alongside your screening rules and the shortlist. It is part of the record of how a decision was made, and it is what you will want when you hire this job again in eight months.
