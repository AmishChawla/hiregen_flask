Getting hiring managers to actually use it

Module 12, Lesson 2  ·  3 min read ·  Updated 21 September 2026

Module 12 · Lesson 2

In short

  • Hiring managers resist for real reasons, not because they dislike change.
  • If they wrote the criteria, the tool is applying their judgement, not replacing it.
  • Show them the people it rejected. That builds trust faster than anything else.

The technology is rarely what stops this working. A hiring manager who does not trust the shortlist will go round the process, and the whole thing quietly becomes optional.

Why they resist, specifically

Not general suspicion of AI. Four concrete worries, and each has a real answer.

  • "It will miss the good ones." Often based on a real memory of a great hire who looked wrong on paper. Take it seriously — it is the same concern this course calls over-filtering.
  • "It does not understand our team." Also fair. It applies the rules it was given, and if those rules came from a generic job description, they do not reflect anything specific.
  • "I will lose control of who I see." The real fear underneath most of the others.
  • "I cannot explain a rejection to someone who asks." Usually unspoken, and entirely reasonable.

What actually works

Make the scorecard theirs. If they wrote the criteria in the intake meeting, the screening is applying their judgement at scale rather than replacing their taste. This one framing does more than everything else combined — and it is why Module 2 comes before Module 4.

Show them the rejections, not the shortlist. Counter-intuitive and very effective. Sit down with the three highest-scoring rejects and ask whether they agree. People trust a system far more once they have seen its mistakes caught — and if they disagree, you have learned something about your rules.

Let them override, and record it. Never fight an override. Record it as a decision with a reason. Overrides are information: three overrides for the same reason means your criteria are wrong, and now you can prove it with their own words.

Show speed early. A shortlist on day two instead of day nine changes the conversation faster than any explanation. Deliver that once and most objections stop being raised.

What does not work

  • Accuracy statistics. Nobody has ever been persuaded by a vendor's numbers.
  • A mandate. Produces compliance in the system and real hiring happening through referrals outside it.
  • Hiding it. They find out, and then the conversation is about trust rather than about hiring.
  • Promising it will find better people. It will not. It reviews everyone consistently and faster, which is a real benefit you can actually deliver.

The one-page brief

Everything a hiring manager needs, in four points. Longer than this does not get read.

  1. These are your criteria, from our intake conversation. Here they are.
  2. Read the groups, not the order. The difference between sixth and seventh is noise. The difference between "strong" and "weak" is not.
  3. If you disagree, say so. I will record it as an override with your reason. Overrides help; they are not failures.
  4. What you produce: scores against the written descriptions, submitted before the debrief.

When someone still will not engage

Run their job the old way and a shadow screening alongside it. Then compare. Either their manual shortlist and the system's largely agree — which settles it — or they differ, and the reasons are a conversation rather than an argument.

Both outcomes are progress. One of them tells you the system is not good enough yet, which is worth knowing before you roll it out further.

The sentence that works

"This applies the criteria you gave me to all 400 applications instead of the 150 I had time to read." It is accurate, it is modest, and it is the actual benefit.
