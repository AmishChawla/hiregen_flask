Setting screening criteria that do not over-filter

Module 4, Lesson 3  ·  5 min read ·  Updated 21 September 2026

Module 4 · Lesson 3

In short

  • A filter removes people. A weighted score just moves them up or down.
  • Only use filters for things you would genuinely reject a great candidate over.
  • Never filter on years of experience, degrees, job titles or career gaps.

Most bad AI screening is not the software's fault. Someone turned a wish list into a set of filters, cut 500 people down to nine, and four of those nine were wrong.

Two kinds of rule

This is the most important idea in the module, and it is simple.

  • A filter removes people. They are gone.
  • A score moves people up or down the list. They are still there.

Filters should only be used for things that are genuinely non-negotiable and easy to check: the legal right to work there, a licence the law requires, a language the job is done in.

Everything else should be a score. Skills, tools, industry background, seniority, education — all of it.

Most tools make everything a filter by default, because filters are easy to build and produce satisfyingly short lists. Resist that. A list of 25 you can skim beats a list of 6 you cannot check, and the skim takes ten minutes.

Six things never to filter on

Do not filter onWhy notDo this instead
Years of experienceBuilt on dates the tool may have read wrongly. Punishes career changersScore it, and stop rewarding extra years past what you need
A degreeWeak predictor for most jobs, and closely tied to backgroundFilter on the skill the degree stands for
Current job titleEvery company names jobs differentlyLook for what they actually did
Gaps in employmentUsually caring, illness, moving country, or redundancyIgnore at this stage. Ask at interview if it matters
Location, for remote jobsYou said it was remoteOnly filter on time zone, and only if the job needs it
Staying a long time at one employerPunishes contractors and agency peopleScore the relevant work, not the shape of the career

Some rules look fair and are not

"Years of experience" feels like a fact. It is not. It is a sum calculated from dates the tool guessed at, using an assumption about what counts as relevant.

"Worked at a similar sized company" sounds neutral. It mostly reflects where someone lives and who they know.

"Steady career progression" rewards people whose lives allowed a straight line.

The test: can you point at the sentence in the CV that proves it, and would two people agree that it does? If not, the rule is doing something other than what its name suggests.

Turning your scorecard into rules

Take the scorecard you built in Module 2. Sort every line into one of three places:

  1. A filter. Three at most. Each has to survive this question: would I really reject an outstanding candidate for missing this?
  2. A score. With a weight, and a note saying what counts as proof.
  3. An interview question. Anything you cannot check from a document. Most personal qualities belong here. Put them in the screening and you are really scoring how well someone writes about themselves.

Writing a "what counts as proof" note

One line per score. Compare these:

RuleWeak noteUsable note
Pipeline experienceHas worked with data pipelinesNames a specific tool and describes what they built with it
Leading a teamLeadership experienceGives a team size, or names people they managed
Client-facingGood with clientsDescribes working with customers outside the company, not colleagues

The usable ones are longer and more specific. That is the point — you can check them against a CV in ten seconds.

Test on 50 before you run on 500

Write your rules. Run them on 50 CVs. Read the top five and the bottom five. Would you have made those calls? Adjust once. Then run the full set.

Two rounds is usually enough. Testing on 50 keeps it quick enough that you will actually do it. The temptation is to go straight to all 500 because the software makes it cheap — but then you are judging 500 results you have already committed to in your head.

You get three filters

If your list of must-haves has more than three items, they are not requirements. They describe a person who does not exist, and you are about to spend six weeks finding that out.
