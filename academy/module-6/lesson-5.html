Rejections that protect your employer brand

Module 6, Lesson 5  ·  3 min read ·  Updated 21 September 2026

Module 6 · Lesson 5

In short

  • Rejection is the part most candidates actually experience. Most companies do it badly.
  • Silence costs you more than a 'no'. People remember being ignored.
  • Reject fast at the start, and personally after an interview.

Rejection is the part of hiring most candidates actually experience, and the part most companies do worst. It is also the cheapest thing you can do for your reputation.

Silence costs more than a no

A rejected candidate is mildly disappointed. An ignored candidate is angry — and the anger goes somewhere: review sites, their network, and their memory when you contact them about a different job in eight months.

That last one is concrete, not vague. Module 5 argued that your own database is your cheapest source of candidates. Ignoring people is how you destroy it. Every silent rejection is a future near-miss who will not reply.

Fast at the start, personal at the end

StageHow fastWhat form
Applied, not progressedWithin daysShort template, no feedback
Screened, not progressedWithin a weekTemplate naming the general reason
InterviewedWithin 2 days of decidingPersonal email, specific and brief
Final stageSame or next dayA call, then written confirmation

Nobody is upset about a template after applying. People are upset about a template after four interviews.

Giving feedback without creating problems

Nervousness about feedback is understandable and usually leads to giving none, which is worse. A workable middle:

  • Tie it to a requirement. "The job needs someone who has run this across multiple regions" is about the job. "You seemed junior" is about the person.
  • Only say things you actually assessed. If it is not on a scorecard, do not say it.
  • Never compare to other candidates. "We went with someone stronger" invites exactly the question you do not want.
  • Stay away from anything near a protected characteristic — including communication style, accent, "presence", and cultural fit.
  • Be consistent. Detailed feedback for some people and none for others is itself a pattern, and patterns get noticed.

If you have nothing evidenced to say, say the decision was close and you do not have detailed feedback. That is honest, and candidates accept it far better than empty phrases.

Keeping the door open, believably

"We'll keep you in mind" means nothing unless you do something. Doing something means: tagging the record against the type of job, noting in one line why they were close, setting a reminder, and telling them explicitly that you will contact them about future jobs — which is also the permission you need to do it.

Then actually do it. A company that follows through on this once gains a candidate who will reply to everything you send for years.

The number worth tracking

What share of candidates got a decision, by stage. Not satisfaction scores — just whether you told them. Most teams find the number is lower than they assumed, and it is the easiest thing on this list to fix.
